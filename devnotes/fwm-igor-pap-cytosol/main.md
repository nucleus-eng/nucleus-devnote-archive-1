abstract: |
  We deploy IGOR, a Bayesian-optimization-backed AI co-scientist from Find What Matters, to optimize the integration of a pyruvate-acetate pathway (PAP) for ATP regeneration into the Cytosol cell-free protein synthesis (CFPS) system. Across four rounds of automated, LabCraft-dispensed reactions, we report on IGOR's empirical performance: it generated mechanistically interpretable yield landscapes, correctly reasoned about aggressive versus conservative optimization goals, salvaged partially compromised plate data through statistical outlier detection, and optimized over a mixed continuous-categorical design space. IGOR-proposed conditions achieved yields on par with an experienced scientist's own designs and, when combined with expert input, outperformed either source alone; conditions generated by an ungrounded general-purpose LLM performed markedly worse. These results illustrate how grounding an AI co-scientist in a physical feedback loop of laboratory measurements can address reliability failure modes that purely literature-driven agents are prone to.

# Introduction

AI co-scientists are multi-agent systems in which large language model (LLM)-backed agents search the literature, generate hypotheses, substantiate their hypotheses with scientific reasoning, and iteratively refine their proposals based upon receiving new information. Such systems promise to compress the time between a research question and an experimentally testable answer. Google's Co-Scientist [[Gottweis *et al.* 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10644-y)], for example, orchestrates specialized Generation, Reflection, Ranking, Evolution, Proximity, and Meta-review agents in a tournament-style "generate, debate, evolve" loop, producing hypotheses that were later supported by laboratory experiments in drug repurposing for acute myeloid leukemia and epigenetic target discovery for liver fibrosis. However, novel hypothesis generation addresses only a small portion of the laboratory workflow. Laboratory researchers generally have a surplus of well-motivated hypotheses emerging from their scientific domain expertise, but what usually consumes their time and budgets is the process of setting up and executing their experiments (including overcoming emergent experimental issues), analyzing and interpreting their results, and iterating until they achieve their scientific goals. What this looks like concretely in the lab: calibrating an instrument, identifying a formulation of biological reagents that yields a target protein, or diagnosing and rectifying sources of experimental variation.

How can an AI co-scientist help with overcoming these experimental challenges? A hint comes from observing what they have in common: 1) they all have quantifiable objectives (e.g., tuning instrument settings to match a reference standard, maximizing protein fluorescence, minimizing measurement noise, etc.); 2) they all require making and executing many interacting decisions (e.g., selecting material precursors, temperature profiles, reagent concentrations, time durations, and instrument settings) in order to achieve those quantifiable objectives; and 3) the size of the decision space (exponentially large in the number of decisions) and the per-experiment cost (large in time, money, and resources) preclude the viability of both trial-and-error and exhaustive search. These three properties define the problem class for which Bayesian optimization (a sequential model-based strategy for global optimization of black-box objective functions) is designed: finding the optimum of an objective that has no closed-form expression, can only be observed by running an (expensive) experiment, and is convolved with observation noise also of an unknown form. An AI co-scientist that incorporates Bayesian optimization reasoning into its generative process would thereby inherit its main benefit as the most experimentally efficient strategy for global optimization [[Brochu *et al.* 2010](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1012.2599)].

Beyond sample efficiency, a Bayesian-optimization-backed AI co-scientist also enables reliability in situations where purely LLM-driven co-scientists are known to struggle. The outputs of LLM-driven co-scientists:

- inherit the weaknesses of their underlying models, including imperfect factuality and hallucination [[Kalai *et al.* 2025](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2509.04664)];
- are subjected to the limitations of the literature they retrieve, including systematic underrepresentation of null results, contaminated reasoning from the presence of erroneous or irreproducible findings, and prevailing assumptions / blind spots within their corpus;
- tend to propagate their own internal biases because, quite often, other LLMs are used as evaluative filters on generative output (based upon characteristics like novelty and utility) [[Gottweis *et al.* 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10644-y)]; and
- systematically deviate from experimental results when tested in the physical world [[Rabanser *et al.* 2026](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/rabanser26a.html)].

All of these challenges contribute to unreliable output as judged by users; in one survey conducted by Anthropic of 80,508 people across 159 countries [[Huang *et al.* 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/features/81k-interviews)], unreliability (hallucinations, inaccuracies, and the resulting verification burden) was the most frequently cited concern, with greater concern expressed by people in higher-stakes professions. Rectifying these reliability concerns is nevertheless possible through access to a Bayesian optimization backend, where the corrective feedback ultimately derives from a quantitative model trained upon iteratively accumulated laboratory evidence that keeps the AI co-scientist grounded in physical reality.

In this work we experimentally deploy such an AI co-scientist to optimize a cell-free protein synthesis (CFPS) process. Using the Iterative Guide and Orchestrated Research (IGOR) platform from Find What Matters, we optimize the formulation for integrating a pyruvate-acetate pathway (PAP) into a cell-free system. The energy cost of sustaining the bioreaction is a major limiting factor in using Cytosol for CFPS, but some of this energy can be provided through the regeneration of ATP via PAP. Such a parallel pathway has been shown to increase protein yield by ~77% when run in parallel with creatine phosphate in the PURE system [[Yadav *et al.* 2025](https://doi.org/10.1021/acssynbio.4c00697)]. Porting PAP into Cytosol is both a route to energetically sustaining CFPS and a concrete high-dimensional design problem (given the many formulation components, mixing steps, and experimental settings involved) that is well suited to IGOR.

# Methods

### Experimental Setup

The full list of components used for Cytosol reactions is enumerated in [Table 1](#table-1). Reagents were assembled as either master mix solutions or individual stock reagents for dispensing via LabCraft [[LabCraft 2026](https://labcraft.bio/products)] automated liquid handler into a small-volume, black 384-well plate for multiplexed 2 µL reactions. Six components were dispensed independently for feature variation. Each component was dispensed via LabCraft at discrete droplets of volume 8–12 nL or 48–87 nL, depending on reagent and dispense mode (CV < 1% per droplet volume measurement). All reactions contained DNA plasmid with mScarlet as the reporter protein for Cytosol transcription/translation performance; the standard deGFP reporter recommended for Cytosol reactions cannot be used here as the flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) component of PAP reactions fluoresces at Ex/Em 450/515 nm.

Iterations of experimental rounds and their associated rationales are described in [Table 2](#table-2). Initial conditions (Round 0) for Cytosol reactions with and without added PAP-pathway components were performed to establish a baseline for the IGOR agent and model inputs. These were determined based on standard Cytosol reaction conditions [[Nucleus, Assemble Base Cytosol 2026](https://docs.nucleus.engineering/docs/processes/assemble-base-cytosol/main/)] and PAP protein and cofactor concentrations determined previously [[Yadav *et al.* 2025](https://doi.org/10.1021/acssynbio.4c00697)]. PAP reagents were added directly on top of base Cytosol with the exception of magnesium acetate (MgOAc) substitution with magnesium glutamate (MgGlu) in the small molecule solution (SMix); creatine kinase (CK) protein was present in the PAP system but this pathway remains inactive in conditions omitting creatine phosphate (CP).

Cytosol reaction performance was measured via mScarlet fluorescence time course readings at 37 °C on a BioTek Cytation3 plate reader for up to 15 h at 5 min intervals (Ex/Em 565/606 nm), with a 5 sec orbital shake at each interval. Each well was normalized against the no-template control to a LabCraft-dispensed 0–4 µM sulforhodamine 101 ladder and kinetic analysis performed with the Nucleus Cell Development Kit (CDK) pipeline [[Nucleus, CDK 2026](https://docs.nucleus.engineering/cdk/cdk-main/)]; [[Newman 2026](https://devnotes.nucleus.engineering/articles/Newman-20260421)]. The estimated parameters from nonlinear curve fits to fluorescence time course readings were used as supervised labels for training IGOR's surrogate model.

### IGOR Setup

IGOR populated its internal corpus of relevant literature with sources that were either autonomously retrieved or explicitly human-provided; the full list is included here [[Berhanu *et al.* 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-09147-4)]; [[Doerr *et al.* 2019](https://doi.org/10.1088/1478-3975/aaf33d)]; [[Koivunen *et al.* 2020](https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0006634)]; [[Lavickova and Maerkl 2019](https://doi.org/10.1021/acssynbio.8b00427)]; [[Li *et al.* 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0106232)]; [[Orbay *et al.* 2016](https://doi.org/10.1088/0960-1317/27/1/015008)]; [[Pandi *et al.* 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-31245-z)]; [[Yadav *et al.* 2025](https://doi.org/10.1021/acssynbio.4c00697)]. Raw data and other supplementary information was provided to IGOR, and the platform's data wrangling capabilities were used to fully curate experimental data; an exportable CSV containing the curated data over all rounds can be found [here](./resources/d3_PAP_r0-r4_261026_IGOR-export-known.csv), with the compiled data [here](./resources/d3_PAP_r0-r4_261026_annotated.csv). The laboratory scientist directly interfaced with IGOR, using the platform dynamically (including through data visualization, evaluation of quantitative analytics, and natural language chat) to plan experiments, troubleshoot ostensible problems with the experimental setup, and biologically interpret iterations of experimental results.

# Results

Characterizing the performance of AI co-scientists is a known challenge; at present, no universally agreed-upon benchmarks exist [[Gottweis *et al.* 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10644-y)]; [[Kapoor *et al.* 2025](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2510.11977)]; [[Mohammadi *et al.* 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3711896.3736570)], and randomized control studies (involving parallel optimization campaigns orchestrated without AI) are cost prohibitive. In light of this, and also in lieu of an exhaustive chronology of all of IGOR's intermediate outputs, here we report on our distilled observations of IGOR's empirical performance over four rounds. Our aims are to clarify (via concrete examples) how IGOR supported experimental execution and to illuminate generalizable lessons for successful AI co-scientist deployment.

### Observation 1: IGOR generated scientific insight and facilitated quantitative interpretation

:name: contour_a

:::

:::{figure} ./figures/contour_r1018_E8.svg
:name: contour_b
As in [](#contour_a), but computed around experiment `r1018_E8`. Note the predicted changes in peak location, height, breadth and shape due to changes in the other parameters (chiefly increased pyruvate).
:::

- Example 1 – Magnesium-phosphate coupling: Both measured and modeled yield evinced non-monotonic dependence on magnesium glutamate, peaking for an intermediate value of around 13 mM with around 17 mM of creatine phosphate (and holding remaining components at the fixed reference conditions of `r1016_E17`, the highest yield reaction in Round 2). This phenomenology matched the scientist's mechanistic expectation of a chelation ridge, in which magnesium is sequestered by phosphorylated species at low concentrations and inhibits translation at high concentrations, leaving a productive window between the two regimes. Subsequent iterations explored this mechanism further to isolate the best phosphate lever—between creatine phosphate and potassium phosphate buffer—to mechanistically support PAP reaction yield increase. IGOR's two-dimensional contour visualization [](#contour_a) displayed to the scientist that these mechanisms had been learned via data alone; being able to corroborate this scientifically valid behavior without explicit intervention in model training nor supplementary prompting increased the scientist's confidence in IGOR's other insights.

  Two other features of the two-dimensional contour visualizations are also noteworthy. First, the principal axes of the elliptical contours are noticeably rotated relative to the magnesium glutamate-creatine phosphate axes; this is direct evidence of a nonlinear interaction between the two components (i.e., the optimal magnesium concentration depends on creatine phosphate, and vice versa), which is emblematic of a typical class of multivariate interdependency in scientific processes. Estimating the magnitudes of these interactions requires a large number of experiments (i.e., exponential in the number of input dimensions) under traditional designs of experiment (e.g., factorial or one-factor-at-a-time) but substantially fewer experiments under multivariate approaches [[Bergstra and Bengio 2012](https://jmlr.org/papers/v13/bergstra12a.html)]. Second, the predicted yield landscape changes noticeably depending upon the specific values that are held fixed for the non-magnesium glutamate, non-creatine phosphate components [](#contour_b). This demonstrates how the magnesium-phosphate coupling itself interacts with the rest of the formulation mixture in order to synthesize the target protein. For the scientist, using IGOR to visualize how these nonlinearities and multivariate interactions affected the predicted yield landscape enhanced their understanding of the CFPS process and greatly informed subsequent planning rounds.

:name: chat
IGOR chat interaction, rerendered as it appeared to the scientist, who accepted the explanation and proceeded with the experimental workflow.

- Example 2 – Natural language justification of experimental recommendations: Every experimental condition that IGOR generates is accompanied by a natural language substantiation of its scientific merit (see [PAP - IGOR round review](./resources/PAP-IGOR-round-review.pdf) and [CDK Steady State Optimization — 10-Experiment STE Engineering Summary](./resources/CDK-Steady-State-Optimization-10-Experiment-STE-Engineering-Summary.pdf)). This matters because even the best recommendations are unlikely to be run unless a scientist can evaluate them on scientific grounds. In the chat interaction displayed in [](#chat), the scientist inquired about a set of IGOR-proposed conditions in which the protein master mix (PMix, together with the PAP enzymes Pox5, AckA, and KatE) was scaled above and below its nominal concentration, asking what the point was of testing `GES-5` at a reduced protein supply and whether testing `GES-13` and `GES-17` at elevated protein might be worthwhile. IGOR responded with a mechanistic rationale rather than a restatement of its predictions: at elevated substrate loads, enzymatic and translational capacity (rather than fuel availability) is expected to become the rate-limiting ceiling, and so supplying 20% additional protein tests whether that extra substrate can actually be converted into protein. This exchange illustrates how the substantiation provided by a useful co-scientist provides the expert a scientific basis on which to commit bench resources to a non-obvious experiment.

### Observation 2: IGOR helped the scientist correctly specify the optimization problem (objective, data admissibility, and search domain)

:::{figure} ./figures/aggression.svg
:name: aggression
Conceptual comparison of a predicted distribution expected by a scientist accustomed to seeking improvement incrementally (orange curve) and a predicted distribution returned by IGOR (purple) for a yield goal of >5. Though the IGOR-generated suggestion is expected to perform worse on average, it has a greater probability than the anticipated suggestion to achieve the specified goal.
:::

- Example 1 – Optimization goal configuration: In Round 1, the scientist specified a yield goal floor of 5.0 standard µM-eq, an aggressive target chosen to push the campaign well beyond its current performance. IGOR responded with suggestions that appeared, on their face, to be poor ones: conditions whose predicted mean yields fell below the goal floor itself, sited at reagent concentrations far outside anything yet tested (100 mM creatine phosphate and 100 mM pyruvate, against observed maxima of 42.4 mM and 54.2 mM). The scientist questioned why the optimizer was proposing experiments it did not expect to reach the target. The explanation is that IGOR maximizes the probability of achieving the goal rather than simply maximizing the predicted mean yield; the two strategies rank candidate experiments differently. [](#aggression) illustrates this for three conditions: the best condition measured so far, an alternative with a slightly higher predicted mean, and a third with a lower predicted mean but substantially greater predictive uncertainty. The probability of realizing the goal is estimated as the area of each predictive distribution overlapping with the target region (and *not* the location of its mean), and by that criterion the third condition is the most likely of the three to succeed. The seemingly unintuitive suggestions were therefore the correct response to the goal as specified: with such an aggressive goal, every candidate's probability of success resided entirely in the upper tail of its predictive distribution. Reaching that far into the tail requires large uncertainty, and large uncertainty is found only outside the sampled region. IGOR explained these dynamics to the scientist, traced the behavior to goal specification rather than to a modeling fault, and then facilitated lowering the yield goal floor to 3.8 µM-eq to better balance the scientist's goal of incremental improvement.

- Example 2 – Admissibility of suspicious data: During the Round 2 run (`r1020`), the PAP protein master mix was observed to contain air bubbles in the dispense reservoir. Because entrained air compromises droplet volume and is not monitored in non-contact dispensing, the conservative and customary response would have been to discard the entire plate and repeat it, forfeiting all 26 conditions along with the bench time and reagents they consumed. Instead, IGOR evaluated the plate statistically, applying a high-statistical-power outlier routine (adapted from [[Rosner 1983](https://doi.org/10.2307/1268549)]) and assessing each well against both its replicates and the trends fit to the rest of the accumulated dataset. Four wells were flagged as inconsistent: `r1020_E6`, `r1020_L9`, `r1020_K14`, and `r1020_I18`. The remaining 22 conditions fit the model's trends consistently, which established that the dispense artifact had been local rather than plate-wide; those 22 were retained and directly informed subsequent rounds. The episode illustrates how IGOR can be used to salvage experimental data that would otherwise be exceedingly difficult for a scientist to discern, with compounding benefits for all ensuing experimentation.

- Example 3 – Optimization over a hybrid continuous-categorical domain: The decisions available to the scientist were not exclusively continuous. Alongside reagent concentrations, the design space contained discrete choices of plasmid backbone, DNA preparation, ribosome lot, and small-molecule mix lot, each capable of shifting the yield response surface. Bayesian optimization as conventionally formulated assumes a continuous, box-bounded domain, and optimizing an acquisition function over the set union of a continuous domain and a finite set of categorical combinations is not an off-the-shelf capability. Because this capability proved necessary for the present deliverable, we developed support for multiple simultaneous categorical features, each admitting multiple categories, and integrated it into IGOR. In this dataset the categorical portion of the domain comprised four features—DNA lot (4 categories), Plasmid (2), Ribosome lot (3), and SMix lot (3)—giving 72 categorical combinations, in addition to the continuous formulation space. That this number remained modest was itself a consequence of the scientist's domain expertise (reflected in assigning constant and non-informative columns to metadata rather than modeling them); however, in general, the combinatorics grow multiplicatively in both the number of categorical features and the number of categories within each. This example reinforces that the search domain is an important part of the optimization problem, and a deployed co-scientist must be able to represent the decisions that a scientist actually faces rather than only those its optimizer natively accommodates.

### Observation 3: IGOR's recommendations achieved parity with expert intuition and marked improvement over ungrounded LLM suggestions

- Example 1 – Round-over-round outcomes: Round 2 (`r1020`) produced the campaign's highest measured yield, with `r1020_F13` reaching steady state at 3.993 µM-eq, reproduced by its replicate `r1020_L15` at 3.724 µM-eq. The two top-performing conditions on that plate, `SY-V18-c20` and `SY-V16-c20` (3.806 µM-eq), were both IGOR literature-guided designs, and control benchmarks across `r1016`, `r1018`, and `r1020` agreed to within plate-level noise, establishing that the gain was attributable to the formulation rather than to a plate offset. The round also yielded a transferable mechanistic finding: moderate creatine phosphate (14–20 mM) combined with robust potassium phosphate (29–44 mM), moderate pyruvate (18–35 mM), and compensated magnesium (15–16.5 mM) consistently reached 3.6–4.0 µM-eq, whereas starving potassium phosphate to 5–10 mM while raising creatine phosphate to 25–38 mM capped yields at 0.84–2.89 µM-eq by limiting acetyl-phosphate flux. Round 3 (`r1021`) regressed to a maximum of 2.53 µM-eq; control samples were similarly reduced, and IGOR traced this to reduced PAP enzyme activity across the plate rather than to the formulations themselves, a diagnosis that preserved the round's design conclusions and informed the decision to re-run its top conditions with freshly prepared protein mix.

:::{figure} ./figures/expression_by_provenance.svg
:name: provenance
Scientist-generated comparison of fluorescence curves and endpoints categorized by experiment provenance. The poor performance of an unassisted general-purpose LLM (green) resulted in the scientist abandoning that source for subsequent experimental rounds.
:::

- Example 2 – Outcome analysis stratified by origin source: Outside of IGOR, the scientist performed an independent quality-control analysis of the Round 1 plate (`r1018`), labeling each condition on the plate by the source that proposed it: the scientist's own domain expertise (MK), IGOR, a general-purpose LLM assistant (Claude), or a hybrid of two sources. Endpoint mScarlet expression, calibrated against the SR101 ladder, was then aggregated by source [](#provenance). Conditions proposed by IGOR (n = 6) reached a mean endpoint of 2.108 µM-eq, effectively indistinguishable from the 2.029 µM-eq achieved by conditions drawn from the scientist's own expertise (n = 13), with comparable medians (2.368 vs. 2.388 µM-eq) and maxima (3.397 vs. 3.531 µM-eq). The two conditions derived jointly by the scientist and IGOR performed best of any class (mean 2.595 µM-eq), exceeding either source acting alone. By contrast, the two IGOR/LLM hybrids averaged 1.313 µM-eq, below either constituent source. The hybrid classes comprise two or three conditions each, so these are not controlled comparisons; nonetheless the separation is large relative to the spread within the better-performing classes. Two conclusions follow. First, IGOR's recommendations were competitive with those of an experienced laboratory scientist and complementary to them, which is the relevant bar for a system intended to augment rather than supplant expertise; the fact that the combination outperformed either source alone is the more consequential result. Second, conditions generated by literature reasoning alone, without grounding in the accumulating experimental record, did not transfer to the bench.

- Example 3 – Tendency towards IGOR usage: While the scientist initially leveraged several generative sources in parallel (including Claude as a representative general-purpose LLM assistant), that practice did not persist over all rounds. From Round 2 onward, the design basis of every round consists of IGOR literature- and model-guided conditions, model-generated experiment suggestions, and agent-guided expert sub-studies, all while excluding the general-purpose LLM. The scientist's mode of interaction with IGOR correspondingly deepened: rather than requesting a batch of conditions and executing it, the scientist used the platform dynamically within each round to interrogate the response surface, challenge individual suggestions, audit plate layouts against dispense constraints, and adjudicate suspect data. We attribute this shift to the properties examined in Observations 1 and 2. A recommendation accompanied by a mechanistic substantiation and a quantified uncertainty, derived from a model trained on the scientist's own measurements, can be evaluated, argued with, and corrected; a recommendation derived from literature-based reasoning alone precludes such engagement, and when it fails at the bench it provides no mechanism by which the next recommendation improves.

### Observation 4: IGOR-supported analyses produce a durable, shareable record of decisions, ideas, outcomes, and distilled learnings

- Beyond all of these specific value propositions, IGOR also delivers overarching value because it preserves, in a contextually linked manner, the relevant literature corpus, the curated data across all rounds, the decision history, the diagnostic analyses, and all natural-language exchanges in one persistently available artifact that is exportable and shareable with collaborators, reviewers, and successors (see the chatlog analysis: [part 1](./resources/D3_chatlog_analysis_part1.pdf), [part 2](./resources/D3_chatlog_analysis_part2.pdf), [full](./resources/D3_chatlog_analysis.pdf); and the [IGOR chat workspace setup log](./resources/IGOR-chat-Workspace-setup-2026-10-27.md)). This artifact produces a fully reproducible and auditable analytical record; maintains failed, discarded, or null results in tandem with experimental breakthroughs; and can be distilled into essential learnings that shape ensuing research.

# Conclusion

Of the four forms of AI co-scientist unreliability discussed in the introduction (namely, hallucination, literature bias, self-propagated bias from LLM evaluators, and systematic deviation from expectation under physical deployment), we collected evidence directly relating to two. Conditions generated by literature reasoning alone resulted in yields roughly an order of magnitude below every other provenance class, which is at once an instance of ungrounded suggestion and a concrete realization of the benchmark-to-bench transfer gap. For the remaining two failure modes, we have described the mechanism and explained how IGOR mitigates them (a surrogate model trained on the laboratory's own measurements cannot inherit literature bias, and dynamic human engagement can interrupt self-propagated LLM bias). Across rounds, we also observed 1) parity between IGOR-proposed conditions and those of an experienced laboratory scientist and 2) superior outcomes from jointly-derived conditions compared to either source acting alone. We expect this complementarity to generalize beyond the field of cell-free protein synthesis. The reliability that IGOR evinces is not a property the system asserts about itself; instead, it emerges from a feedback loop in which every new piece of information physically grounds subsequent analysis and in which scientific judgment ultimately remains with the expert scientist.
